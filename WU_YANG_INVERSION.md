# Wu–Yang Inversion and Density-Difference Optimization

Given a reference density $n(\mathbf{r})$, the goal of PDFT inversion is to determine a partition potential $v_p(\mathbf{r})$ such that the fragment density satisfies

$$n_f(\mathbf{r}; v_p) = n(\mathbf{r}).$$

We define the constrained functional

$$L[v_p] = E_f[n_f] + \int d\mathbf{r}\, v_p(\mathbf{r}) \left( n_f(\mathbf{r}) - n(\mathbf{r}) \right),$$

where $E_f$ is the fragment energy functional and $n_f$ is the fragment density generated by the partition potential $v_p$.

The stationary condition is

$$\frac{\delta L}{\delta v_p(\mathbf{r})} = n_f(\mathbf{r}) - n(\mathbf{r}).$$

Thus, the density difference

$$g(\mathbf{r}) = n_f(\mathbf{r}) - n(\mathbf{r})$$

acts as the optimization gradient.

## Potential Basis Representation

In practice, the partition potential is expanded in a potential basis set (PBS),

$$v_p(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_t b_t\, g_t(\mathbf{r}),$$

where $g_t(\mathbf{r})$ are PBS basis functions and $b_t$ are the variational coefficients.

The gradient with respect to the PBS coefficients is

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial b_t} = \int d\mathbf{r}\, g_t(\mathbf{r}) \left( n_f(\mathbf{r}) - n(\mathbf{r}) \right).$$

Defining

$$\Delta n(\mathbf{r}) = n_f(\mathbf{r}) - n(\mathbf{r}),$$

the gradient can be written compactly as

$$(\nabla L)_t = \langle g_t, \Delta n \rangle .$$

## Density-Difference Update

A simple density-difference optimization step is

$$b_t^{k+1} = b_t^k + \lambda \frac{\partial L}{\partial b_t},$$

or equivalently in real space,

$$v_p^{k+1}(\mathbf{r}) = v_p^k(\mathbf{r}) + \lambda \left( n_f(\mathbf{r}) - n(\mathbf{r}) \right),$$

where $\lambda$ is the step size.

## Wu–Yang Hessian

The Wu–Yang method uses the second functional derivative

$$H_{tt'} = \frac{\partial^2 L}{\partial b_t \partial b_{t'}},$$

which is related to the Kohn–Sham density response function,

$$\chi(\mathbf{r}, \mathbf{r}') = \frac{\delta n(\mathbf{r})}{\delta v(\mathbf{r}')}.$$

The Newton update is then

$$\mathbf{b}^{k+1} = \mathbf{b}^k - H^{-1} \nabla L.$$

To improve numerical stability, a regularized form is often used,

$$\mathbf{b}^{k+1} = \mathbf{b}^k - (H + \eta I)^{-1} \nabla L,$$

where $\eta$ is a regularization parameter.

## Convergence Metrics

The optimization can be monitored using the gradient norm,

$$\|g\| = \left( \sum_t g_t^2 \right)^{1/2},$$

the density $L^1$ error,

$$\Delta n_{L1} = \int d\mathbf{r}\, |n_f(\mathbf{r}) - n(\mathbf{r})|,$$

and the electron-number deviation,

$$\Delta N = \int d\mathbf{r}\, (n_f(\mathbf{r}) - n(\mathbf{r})).$$

For a converged inversion, $\Delta N$ should approach zero.
